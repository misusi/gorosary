## The Liturgical Year

The Church does not run its year from January to December. It runs it on the
life of Jesus Christ. The year opens with the first Sunday of Advent, and goes
on through Christmas, through the forty days of the desert in Lent, through
the Paschal Triduum and the fifty days of Easter, and through the long
Ordinary Time that follows, until Advent comes round again. This is the
liturgical year, and its shape is the shape of salvation: the Son of God
comes, is born, dies, rises, and sends His Spirit, and the Church walks
through all of it with Him.

Inside that year there are two kinds of days. The **movable** days move with
Easter, because Easter moves: Lent, Holy Week, Easter, the Ascension,
Pentecost, Trinity Sunday, Corpus Christi, and Christ the King. The **fixed**
days keep the same date every year, whatever the date of Easter: Christmas on
the twenty-fifth of December, the Assumption on the fifteenth of August, the
Immaculate Conception on the eighth of December.

## Solemnities, Feasts, and Memorials

The Church ranks its days, and the rank decides what is sung, what is said,
and how much is given to God on that day.

- **Solemnity.** The highest rank. These are the days on which the whole
  Church gathers: the solemnities of Christ (Christmas, Easter, the Ascension,
  Pentecost, Trinity Sunday, Corpus Christi, Christ the King, the
  Transfiguration, the Holy Family) and of the Blessed Virgin Mary (Mary,
  Mother of God; the Assumption; the Immaculate Conception). The Mass is of
  the solemnity itself, with the Gloria, and the Creed is said.
- **Feast.** A day of more than ordinary rank, but below a solemnity, given
  either to a saint or to a particular mystery of the Lord: the Nativity of
  the Blessed Virgin Mary, the Exaltation of the Holy Cross, the Apostles, the
  Martyrs. The Mass is of the feast, and the Creed is not said.
- **Memorial.** An obligatory commemoration of one saint, or of a group of
  saints. The Mass is of the memorial, unless a Sunday or a feast of greater
  rank falls on the same day, in which case that day is kept instead.
- **Optional memorial.** A saint whose day the Church proposes but does not
  oblige. Where an obligatory memorial falls on the same day, the optional
  one is not celebrated; where a feast falls on the same day, the optional
  memorial is usually omitted altogether.
- **Commemoration.** A day added by a national or diocesan calendar, or a
  commemoration the Church once kept and no longer keeps in the General Roman
  Calendar, but which is still treasured in many places.

Every saint has his place in the calendar for two reasons: the date on which
the Church has traditionally kept his feast, and the way in which he served.
Saints who died for the faith are kept as martyrs. Saints who gave their
lives to the poor, to the sick, to the truth, or to the Cross are kept as
confessors. The Church does not forget its own.

## The Colours of the Church

The colour of the vestments tells you what kind of day it is, and what the
Church is doing that day.

- **White.** The colour of light and of joy, worn when Christ is revealed. It
  is worn at Christmas, at Easter, at the Transfiguration, and at the feasts
  of Christ and of the saints whose holiness shines out. It is the colour of
  the Easter Vigil, and of the weddings and baptisms the Church is happy to
  surround with light.
- **Red.** The colour of fire and of blood. It is worn at Pentecost, for the
  martyrs, and for the Passion of the Lord. Palm Sunday already wears red,
  because the Passion has begun.
- **Green.** The colour of life and of hope, worn throughout Ordinary Time,
  the long season in which the Church grows, marries, works, and waits.
- **Violet.** The colour of penance and of preparation, worn in Advent and in
  Lent and on the Rogation days. Lighter shades, called rose, are used on the
  Third Sunday of Advent and the Fifth Sunday of Lent, when the joy of the
  season cannot be hidden altogether.
- **Black.** The colour of mourning, worn for the Masses for the Dead on All
  Souls' Day and on the days following, and on Good Friday. White takes its
  place at the Mass of the Vigil of All Saints, and at funerals.
- **Gold.** Used on the principal solemnities of the Lord and of the Blessed
  Virgin Mary, as befits days of the greatest dignity.

## Fasts and Abstinences

The Church fasts as well as feasts. Fasting is not a punishment; it is the
old way of saying that God comes first.

- **Fridays and Wednesdays of Lent.** Days of fast and abstinence. The Church
  fasts twice a week in the desert season, as the Saviour Himself did.
- **Fridays of the year.** Days of abstinence. The Church asks for a small
  sacrifice rather than a great one: no meat, and, where it can be managed, no
  second helping.
- **The Ember Days.** The first full week after the first Sunday of Lent, the
  week after Pentecost, the third week of September, and the week before the
  third Sunday of Advent. On these days the Church keeps its ancient
  threefold discipline: fasting, abstinence from meat, and prayer for the
  Church, for those in need, and for the dead.
- **The Vigils.** The day before Christmas, the day before the Annunciation,
  the day before Pentecost, the day before the Assumption, and the day before
  All Saints. The Christmas Vigil is a day of fast and abstinence.
- **Abstinence.** The Church does not forbid food and drink. It asks for
  less. A fast is one meal in the day, with no meat; abstinence is the
  giving up of meat alone. Since 1971 the Church has asked for abstinence
  rather than a fast on ordinary Fridays.
- **The fast before Holy Communion.** At least one hour before receiving
  Holy Communion, and, where it can be observed without difficulty, three
  hours' abstinence from food and drink, water excepted.

## The Great Jubilee and Transferred Feasts

- **The Great Jubilee.** Every twenty-five years the Church proclaims a Great
  Jubilee: a year of grace in which the ordinary rules of fasting are relaxed
  and the ordinary penalties of sin are set aside, so that the faithful may
  begin again. The Jubilee of 2000 was the last of the extraordinary
  jubilees; the Jubilee of 2025 was the first ordinary Jubilee of the modern
  era, and its gifts were meant to be shared for years afterwards.
- **Transferred feasts.** A solemnity or a feast that falls on a Sunday, or
  on a day of greater rank, is moved to the nearest free day, so that the
  great days of the year are not crowded out. A solemnity that falls on a
  Sunday in Lent is moved to a Saturday before the Triduum, so that the
  solemnities of Holy Week and of Easter are never displaced. When a feast
  falls on a Sunday, the feast is celebrated on that Sunday in place of the
  Sunday Mass, and the Sunday Mass is said on the Monday following. The rules
  for these transfers were tightened in 2023, and in every case the intent is
  the same: that the greatest days should be kept whole.

## The Principal Days of the Year

**Fixed dates**

- 1 January - Mary, Mother of God (solemnity)
- 6 January - The Epiphany of the Lord (solemnity)
- 19 March - St. Joseph, Spouse of the Blessed Virgin Mary (solemnity)
- 25 March - The Annunciation of the Lord (solemnity)
- 15 August - The Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary (solemnity)
- 1 November - All Saints (solemnity)
- 2 November - All Souls (solemnity)
- 8 December - The Immaculate Conception of the Blessed Virgin Mary (solemnity)
- 25 December - The Nativity of the Lord (solemnity)

**Movable feasts, which follow Easter**

- The Baptism of the Lord - the Sunday after the Epiphany, or 6 January where
  Epiphany is kept on its own day
- Ash Wednesday - seven weeks before Easter; the first day of Lent, and a fast
  day
- Palm Sunday - the Sunday before Easter, when the Lord enters Jerusalem
- Holy Thursday - the evening of the Lord's Supper
- Good Friday - the Passion and death of the Lord
- Holy Saturday - the Lord lies in the tomb
- Easter Sunday - the Resurrection of the Lord, the greatest of all the days
- The Ascension - the fortieth day of Easter
- Pentecost - the fiftieth day of Easter, when the Holy Spirit comes
- Trinity Sunday - the Sunday after Pentecost
- Corpus Christi - the Thursday after Trinity Sunday
- Christ the King - the last Sunday of Ordinary Time

And between the fixed and the movable days there stands the whole calendar of
the saints, which is set out elsewhere in this section.
