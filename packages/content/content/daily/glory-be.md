## The Doxology

> Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit. As it was in
> the beginning, is now, and will be forever. Amen. Amen.

Many households and prayer books give only a single *Amen* at the end. Both
forms are traditional, and both are said in the Church.

## When It Is Said

- **After each decade of the Rosary.** This is its most familiar use. After the Our Father and the ten Hail Marys of a decade, the Glory Be is said once, and the mystery is pondered.
- **After the Our Father**, in the place where the Praise stands at the head of the prayer. The Preface of the Mass is a hymn of praise to God the Father sung at the opening of the Canon, and the doxology takes that place of praise wherever the Preface is not sung.
- **At the end of a prayer.** Very many traditional prayers close with the Glory Be, and the **Sign of the Cross** is made with it.
- **In the Rosary itself**, after the three Hail Marys of the beginning, and at the close of each decade.
- After the **Hail Mary**, at the close of the **Angelus** and the **Regina Coeli**.

## The Variations

There are two forms in common use.

The **full doxology**, with the whole text given above, is used at the end of
each decade of the Rosary and at the close of the **Gloria** of the Mass, where
it is sung in Latin as the *Gloria in excelsis* is not — for the *Gloria in
excelsis* is a different hymn altogether, taken from Luke and sung at the
opening of Mass.

The **short form** is this:

> Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit.

This is the form that is said in the **Mass** after the Creed and after the
homily, and at the end of the Prayer of the Faithful. In the Roman Missal of
1969 this short doxology was left out; it is found in the Missal of 1962, and
it has found its way back into the mouths of the faithful.

## The Scriptural Roots

The doxology is not a human invention. The older books show its lineage
plainly.

St. Paul ends his letter to the Romans with a doxology: *to Him be glory for
ever and ever. Amen.* (Romans 16:27)

And in his letter to the Ephesians, he speaks of the glory of God in the same
strain: that we may know *what is the exceeding greatness of His power in us
and in the Church, according to the working of His mighty strength*, in the
same Church that is called *the whole body, fitted together, and compacted in
himself* (Ephesians 1:18-19; and 4:16).

The form of the doxology that the Church uses was framed in the fourth and
fifth centuries, in the same period as the Creed, and it took its shape from
these older formulas of praise. The custom of ending each decade of the Rosary
with it is of long standing, and it was encouraged in the Church in the
nineteenth century.

## A Note

The Glory Be is said aloud, and not thought. It is praise offered to God
directly, without asking for anything, and it is the one place in the Rosary
where the person praying speaks to God for His own sake alone. Sound it once
more than feels natural. Praise ought to be heard.
