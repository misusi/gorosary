## The Prayer

> Hail Mary, full of grace, the Lord is with thee. Blessed art thou among women,
> and blessed is the fruit of thy womb, Jesus. Holy Mary, Mother of God, pray
> for us sinners, now, and at the hour of our death. Amen.

## The Liturgical Commentary

The Hail Mary is the longest of the short prayers, and the only one that the
Church has built out of the words of Scripture and its own supplication. It is
said at the Rosary, at the Angelus, at the end of every decade of the Divine
Mercy Chaplet, and at the devotions of the Rosary hour.

## 1. Hail Mary, full of grace

> Hail Mary, full of grace.

These are the words of the Archangel Gabriel, spoken in the house of Nazareth
in the city of Galilee, in the month of March. *Hail* is the greeting of a
messenger sent from God. *Full of grace* means that Mary is filled with the
grace of God — the grace of God is not in her as a decoration, but is her very
life, and she has no life of her own apart from it. To be greeted in this way
is to be greeted by God Himself.

## 2. The Lord is with thee

> The Lord is with thee.

This is the second half of the angel's greeting, and it is the ground of the
first. It was not the angel's own word that made Mary full of grace; the Lord
was with her, and therefore grace was in her. The greeting is the angel's
form of words, but the thing it announces is the presence of God, which is
older than the angel and greater than the message.

## 3. Blessed art thou among women

> Blessed art thou among women.

These are the words of Elizabeth, spoken to Mary when the two women met, and
they are the Church's greeting to Mary in the second of its two parts. The
prophets had said that the Messiah would be blessed; here it is said plainly
of the mother, and the Church has never taken it back. The greeting is not
only for Mary, but for every mother who carries the life of Christ in her
children.

## 4. And blessed is the fruit of thy womb

> And blessed is the fruit of thy womb, Jesus.

This is the end of Elizabeth's greeting, and the Angel gave its meaning before
she did: *thou shalt call His name Jesus*. The blessing of the mother is the
blessing of the Son, and the prayer that follows is not for Mary alone but for
the Word made flesh in her.

## 5. Holy Mary, Mother of God

> Holy Mary, Mother of God.

Here the prayer passes from Scripture into the prayer of the Church. *Mother of
God* is the title given at the Council of Ephesus in 431, and it is the title
that tells us who it is whose mother she is. To call her the Mother of God is
to confess that her Son is God. The Church says this aloud because it is a
matter of faith, and not of sentiment.

## 6. Pray for us sinners

> Pray for us sinners.

Everything before this is Scripture and Creed; everything after it is the
Church asking. The Church does not pray to Mary as to God, nor does it ask
her for anything that is God's to give. It asks her intercession — the
grace of her prayers with Jesus Christ, who is her Son, and who is the one
Mediator between God and men. *Sinners* is said in the plural because the
prayer is the prayer of a whole Church that knows itself to be in need of
mercy.

## 7. Now, and at the hour of our death

> Now, and at the hour of our death. Amen.

The prayer is not only for the present but for the last moment, which is the
hour in which everything is settled. The older devotional books make much of
the two words *now* and *at the hour of our death*: the Church asks Mary to
stand with her now, in the ordinary middle of life, and at the end of it. The
*Amen* seals the prayer with the agreement of the whole Church speaking it.

## The History of the Prayer

The Hail Mary is built from two parts, and it is worth seeing where each comes
from.

The **first part** is the greeting of the Angel: *Hail Mary, full of grace,
the Lord is with thee.* It is the word of Gabriel in the Gospel of St. Luke,
in chapter one, and it was offered to Mary by the Church from very early on
as a salutation.

The **second part** is the Church's own prayer, and it is built out of the
same chapter of St. Luke. It begins with Elizabeth's greeting, which the older
books call the canticle of the Visitation, and it goes on to the title *Mother
of God* fixed by the Council of Ephesus, and it closes with the plea of the
Church for its own sins.

The two parts came together gradually, as the Church learned to address the
Blessed Virgin in the words that Scripture had given it, and as it learned to
ask her help. The prayer in its present form was in common use by the
thirteenth century, and it is said, in one form or another, in every Catholic
church in the world.

## A Note

The Hail Mary is a Scripture prayer, and it is said with Scripture. The older
books advise that the three Hail Marys of the Rosary should be said slowly
enough to be meditated upon, one article of the Creed in each — and the same
advice is good for the Hail Mary itself.
