## The Creed as Recited in the Mass

This is the Creed of the Apostles, said at Mass on Sundays and on the other
days appointed for it, and it is the Creed of the Catechism.

> I believe in one God, the Father almighty, maker of heaven and earth, of all
> things visible and invisible. I believe in one Lord, Jesus Christ, the only
> Son of God, who was conceived by the Holy Spirit and was born of the Virgin
> Mary, who suffered under Pontius Pilate, was crucified, died, and was buried.
> He descended into hell. On the third day He rose again from the dead. He
> ascended into heaven and is seated at the right hand of God the Father
> almighty. He will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead. I
> believe in the Holy Spirit, the holy catholic Church, the communion of
> saints, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body, and the life
> everlasting. Amen.

## The Creed of the Councils

On the other days of the year, the Creed sung at Mass is the Creed of Nicaea and
Constantinople, which sets out the doctrine of the Church in fuller form:

> I believe in one God, the Father almighty, maker of heaven and earth, of all
> things visible and invisible.
>
> I believe in one Lord, Jesus Christ, the only Son of God, who was conceived by
> the Holy Spirit, was incarnate by the Holy Spirit of the Virgin Mary, and was
> made man. For us men, and for our salvation, He came down from heaven, and
> was incarnate by the Holy Spirit of the Virgin Mary, and was made man; and
> was crucified also for us, under Pontius Pilate; was buried, and descended
> into hell. The third day He rose again from the dead. He ascended into
> heaven, and sitteth at the right hand of God the Father almighty. He shall
> come again in glory to judge the living and the dead. Whose kingdom shall
> have no end.
>
> And I believe in the Holy Spirit, the Lord and giver of life, who proceeds
> from the Father and the Son, who with the Father and the Son is worshiped and
> glorified, who spoke by the prophets. And I believe in one holy catholic
> Church. I acknowledge one baptism for the remission of sins. I look for the
> resurrection of the dead, and the life of the world to come. Amen.

## The History of the Creed

The Apostles' Creed is called the Creed of the Apostles because it is built
upon the teaching of the Apostles and upon the confession that they made in
baptism. It is not the work of any one of them, and it was not written down
all at once. It grew, slowly, out of the preaching of the Apostles and out of
the questions put to converts before they were admitted to the Church.

The oldest form that survives in a written book is the one found in the
Sacramentary of Gellone, in Gaul, of the eighth century; it already contains
the words *He descended into hell*, which the older form had lacked. The
Creed in use in the Roman Church was substantially fixed in the form we
recite today by the typical edition of the Roman Missal of **Pius XI**, in
**1934**, which restored the ancient phrases *He descended into hell* and *the
life everlasting*.

The **Creed of the Councils** came out of the great ecumenical councils of the
fourth and fifth centuries, which defined the doctrine the Church believes:

- **Nicaea**, in 325, condemned the denial of the divinity of the Son and set down the form of the Creed that bears its name.
- **Constantinople**, in 381, completed that Creed, adding the articles concerning the Holy Spirit and the Church.
- **Ephesus**, in 431, defined the unity of Christ and the office of Mary as Mother of God.
- **Chalcedon**, in 451, set down the one Christ in two natures, confessed without confusion, without change, without division, and without separation.

The Council of Trent, in the sixteenth century, declared the Nicene Creed to be
the Creed of the Roman Catholic Church, so that nothing essential to the
faith might be added to it and nothing belonging to the faith might be taken
away from it.

## A Note

The Creed is said standing, and it is said together. It is the one prayer of
the Mass that belongs to the people as well as to the priest, and no one in
the Church has ever been excused from it.
